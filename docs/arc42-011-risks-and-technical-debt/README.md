# Risks and technical debt

The outstanding work, as numbered items. Each item is a directory holding a
README whose first line is the item's state, and whatever the item needs
beside it: a measurement, a listing, a trace. A resolved item is deleted;
the commit that resolved it, or the decision record it produced, is its
record.

A risk that has not become an item is listed under Risks below, in one
sentence, until it either becomes one or stops being a risk.

## What an item carries

An issue says what is to be done and whether it is done. The specification
says how the store works once it is. An item carries what neither holds: the
problem as it stands, the order the steps become possible in, what was
decided along the way and what bit. It names the issue holding the work by
address, because a number is not something a reader here can open.

A useful item answers, in this order: what this is, where it stands, the
sequence, the decisions and why each beat the alternative, the traps, what
is deliberately not being done, and the commands that prove it.

| [032 One lane for the fleet, and two levels of step](032-one-lane-for-the-fleet/README.md) | Open, and not started, with an eight-step plan proposed rather than agreed and the desired state described beside the concept it grows out of. A step is to be defined once and perform for every tenant, subscribed by step and never by tenant, with work joined from the tenant stores into the managing tenant's durable layer as one stream and results split back out — the managing tenant a carrier that reads manifests and holds no key. A unified step keeps nothing in memory, its execution state living in the substrate, and feedback comes home on a channel of its own so the tenant's own record stays the answer to what is true now. A step that reads only the envelope is a router; asking for a payload makes it a data processor, derived rather than declared, with an entry in that tenant's trail naming the processor. Tenant-level steps over HTTP stay, for another organisation's system and a tenant's own appliance, so there are two levels rather than one. Much of the model already exists — three carriers a runner cannot tell apart, sealing to an enrolled participant, a managing tenant, disclosure with a reason — and the item is the delta plus six decisions, three of them now settled in shape. Enrolment is per tenant, because enrolling once at fleet level means something re-seals and therefore holds tenant keys. A tenant admits most application-level steps and cannot refuse a few, which is an agreement signed by joining rather than a per-step choice — and the word cannot be `mandatorySteps`, which already means the tenant's own direction and classifies incidents. And what a tenant reads is a register of processing, one row per step that OPENS a payload, with a carrier absent from it: the trail is recorded regardless, so the register is what the code said and the trail is what it did, and a disagreement is an incident rather than a refusal, because an enrolled application holds the key and no cryptography can stop a processor processing. A deployment also states what happens to work whose processing is not yet approved — applied under the agreement, processed and named, or not processed until approved, with a bounded window as the middle two in sequence — and refusal is real there, because approval is known before the payload is sealed. Processed-and-named is the default and a row may state its own, so a new row can halt without stopping everything else — which leaves the incident carrying the weight, and it has to clear on approval and say how long a row has gone unapproved, or the default is not honest. A tenant's own steps stay in its own tenant, on the durable layer it already launches at bring-up, consumed over HTTP by remote participants that were polling a door anyway — so they cost no substrate and the joiner simply does not lift their runs. Application-level steps are declared in the management tenant's descriptor — which already exists, is already a tenant spec and already carries a steps field the sample leaves empty — so declaring one is what prepares its queues and their substrate, the level invariant becomes checkable where it is written, and the register a tenant reads has one source. An entry needs more than a tenant's does (which slots it opens, required or admitted, its posture, where its queue lives), which argues for its own key rather than widening `steps`. Where the claim lives is dissolved rather than answered: a step code must belong to exactly one level, and then each side settles its own — so the work is that invariant, and the joiner claims nothing because observing is not claiming. The durable layer was read rather than assumed, and it does not have the shape the design first took: its queues are POLLED at an interval each names, default one second, with backoff that is a brake on a failing database rather than idle drift; notification is three channels whose names are library constants, so a channel per step cannot exist and every process in a database hears every notification and discards what is not its own; `partitionQueue` divides concurrency accounting by workflow class, not consumers; and the whole mechanism can be switched off, so nothing may depend on a wake-up arriving. A notify-driven step partition is therefore a long-lived workflow per step — the shape of a tenant's door on the stream, re-keyed — while a queue per step costs a second of latency and far less machinery, which is the version to build first. A database per step is the likely production answer rather than a last resort, because the expected load is few steps each carrying every tenant's work — the profile where the benefit scales with load per step and the cost with their number — and it buys contention isolation on the durable layer's own hot tables as much as notification isolation. Its cost is one permanently held listener connection and a pool per participating process, which is small where a worker application serves one step and large in an all-in-one server, so placement is configuration rather than structure: a step names its substrate and several may share one. These databases are runtime-owned and are not tenants — a durable bootstrap and nothing else. Much of the input side already exists: a work-domain observer is a durable named consumer over every tenant, and it cannot reach payloads because the work stream carries the run and not the record. Withdrawing an application-level step closes it to new work and removes nothing: what is queued is still performed, and the substrate is removed by a person rather than by the sweep, because a configuration change that discarded runs would be the store losing work to tidy itself up. That is safe because a joined item is a copy — the run is a record in the tenant that authored it — so removing a substrate destroys copies and no records, and a tenant sees its runs stop progressing, which is the state they are in. Left open: what the reduced account holds, a joined item's lifetime, how fine a register row is, and whether "consent" is the right word for a tenant's authorisation rather than a data subject's basis. Item 031 is a prerequisite. |
